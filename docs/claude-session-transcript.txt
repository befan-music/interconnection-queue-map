> how can we make a business model out of a map=

● A map on its own is hard to sell. We rebuilt this one from public data in a
  day, so a funded competitor can too, and grid operators now publish free
  heatmaps under FERC Order 2023. What people pay for is a decision they can act
  on, backed by data others don't have. So give the map away to attract users
  and charge for the answers built on top of it.

  Who would pay, and for what

  Customer: Renewable and storage developers
  Their pain: Wasted land options and deposits on sites that turn out congested.

  CAISO no longer issues pre-application reports, and IPE 5.0 doubles deposits.
  What they'd buy: Site screening reports, a watchlist of substations, alerts
  when the queue at a substation changes
  Pricing model (hypothesis to test): Per-report fee plus a subscription
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Customer: Data-center and large-load developers
  Their pain: Getting power connected is the bottleneck, and they need to know
  where a large load can connect
  What they'd buy: A load-headroom ranking of substations near fiber, water and
  suitable land
  Pricing model (hypothesis to test): Highest willingness to pay; enterprise
  contracts
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Customer: Investors, banks, private equity
  Their pain: Checking whether a project they'd fund or buy can realistically
  connect
  What they'd buy: A "queue risk audit" per project or portfolio
  Pricing model (hypothesis to test): Per transaction, plus an annual licence
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Customer: Off-takers (CCAs, corporate buyers)
  Their pain: Signing power contracts with projects that sit in the queue for
  years
  What they'd buy: A deliverability score for each project pitched to them
  Pricing model (hypothesis to test): Subscription
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Customer: Utilities and grid operators
  Their pain: Speculative applications and repeated restudies
  What they'd buy: Pre-screening tools for incoming applications
  Pricing model (hypothesis to test): Slow to sell; later

  Revenue options, ranked by how fast they could make money

  1. Pay-per-site screening report. This fills the gap CAISO's pre-application
     reports left. A developer enters a substation or a land parcel and gets
     back capacity after the queue, the line that limits it, the queue ahead of
     them, and a risk score. It's easy to buy without a long sales process, and
     it tests demand quickly.
  2. Subscription (free, pro, enterprise). The free national map is the
     marketing. Pro adds filters, exports, alerts and history. Enterprise adds
     an API and portfolio views.
  3. Due-diligence reports. Fewer customers, but each deal is larger and banks
     and funds can pay.
  4. Data licensing and API. Sell the cleaned national queue and capacity data
     to analytics platforms and funds.
  5. Marketplace (later). Match developers with landowners near green
     substations, or broker spare connection capacity at existing plants. FERC
     lets existing plants offer surplus interconnection service, which is a
     natural fit for adding storage. Take a success fee.

  Where the defensible advantage comes from

  The public layer is replicable. These assets wouldn't be:
  - Network-upgrade costs, extracted from ISO cluster-study reports. Those PDFs
    list the upgrades and costs assigned to each project. Turning them into a
    dataset of $/kW by connection point is tedious work that most competitors
    won't do.
  - Completion odds, learned from LBNL's ~24,000 withdrawn requests instead of
    my fixed assumptions.
  - Validation against real data. Customers who have study results can confirm
    or correct the model, and every correction improves it.
  - Utility study files under NDA. These give real ratings and outage cases.
    They can sharpen your scores, but the redistribution rules are strict and
    need a lawyer's review.

● The short version

  The two ideas sit at different points of the same problem. The map finds the
  bottlenecks; reconductoring removes them. The map is easier to start and
  cheaper to run, but easy to copy. Reconductoring touches billions in grid
  spending and solves the problem itself, but as a pure services company it is
  hard, slow and low-margin.

  The strongest company combines them: use the map to find and package
  reconductoring projects, and get paid when capacity is unlocked.

  What our model says about the reconductoring opportunity

  I queried the model we built. It's a screening model, so treat these as
  directional numbers:
  - 393 line segments (about 18,800 km) are the binding limit for substations
    holding ~890 GW of queued projects.
  - Of these, 316 are limited by heat rather than stability, and are under 80
    km. That is about 7,600 km of line in front of ~630 GW of queue. These are
    the lines where new conductors actually add capacity. Long lines limited by
    stability don't benefit.
  - The top candidates match known trouble spots:
    - Odessa 345 kV in West Texas, with ~36 GW queued behind it
    - Holcomb 345 kV in Kansas, ~29 GW
    - Midway–Lincoln 230 kV in Colorado, ~10 GW
    - Midlothian–Carson 500 kV in Virginia (Dominion), ~9 GW
    - Southern California 500 kV lines: Lugo–Victorville and Red Bluff–Devers

  So the demand is concentrated: a few hundred line segments hold back most of
  the queue. That concentration is what makes a reconductoring business
  possible.

  Ways to make reconductoring a business

  1. Construction contractor. You put up the new wire yourself. You'd compete
     with Quanta, MYR and MasTec, and need crews, equipment, a safety record and
     years of vendor qualification. That's a real business but not a startup.
     Avoid it.
  2. Conductor manufacturer. CTC Global (ACCC) and TS Conductor already do this,
     and it is capital-heavy. Better as a partner than a business to start.
  3. Engineering and analytics: the "reconductoring developer" (recommended
     core). Screen every line, then do the engineering on the shortlist: sag and
     clearance, tower loading, outage plans. Deliver the business case a
     utility can file.
     - Utilities increasingly have to run these evaluations. FERC Order 1920
       requires advanced conductors to be considered in long-term planning.
       California's SB 1006 requires utilities to assess which lines can be
       reconductored. Virginia and Minnesota require advanced technologies in
       resource plans, and Montana's HB 729 offers a higher rate of return for
       advanced conductors.
     - Revenue comes from study fees.
  4. Developer-funded upgrade orchestration (the biggest opportunity and the
     closest fit with the map). Developers stuck behind a constraint such as
     Odessa 345 kV are the ones assigned the cost of network upgrades in cluster
     studies.
     - You'd organise them, propose reconductoring as a cheaper and faster
       alternative, and coordinate the funding. The utility still owns and
       builds the line.
     - You'd earn a success fee per MW unlocked. For example, if reconductoring
       one constraint unlocks 1 GW and you charge $10/kW, that's $10M. This is a
       hypothesis to test, not a market rate.
  5. Owning or financing lines. In most US markets a third party can't easily
     own pieces of a utility's network, so partner with utilities rather than
     own lines.

  Why now, and what could stop it

  Tailwinds:

> what does this mean:
  Ignores N-1 contingencies

● It means the tool calculates grid capacity as if every line and transformer is
  always in service, without checking what happens when one of them fails.

  What N-1 means. Grid operators must plan so the grid stays safe if any single
  element fails, such as one line, one transformer or one generator. "N" is all
  the elements in the grid, and "N-1" is the grid with one of them out. Every
  interconnection study checks this: a new power plant is only allowed if no
  line overloads, even with any single element out.

  Why ignoring it matters. Take a substation connected by two lines of 200 MW
  each:
  - Without N-1: both lines are in service, so it looks like there's room for
    about 400 MW.
  - With N-1: if one line trips, all the power must flow over the other, so the
    real safe limit is about 200 MW.

  A tool that ignores N-1 can therefore overstate available capacity, sometimes
  by half or more. A developer might see "green" where the utility's study would
  later say "red" or require expensive upgrades.

  Context for that claim:
  - It comes from PVcase, a competitor of LandGate, in a comparison article, so
    it's a vendor's claim and not independently verified.
  - Our own map doesn't run a full N-1 analysis either. It approximates it in
    two ways: it only uses 85% of each line's rating as a safety margin, and it
    caps each substation at its N-1 branch capacity (the sum of its lines minus
    the largest one). That is why it's a screening tool, not a study. The README
    lists this as a known limitation.

> what does injection refer to in our context?

● Injection means power flowing into the grid at a specific point. A solar farm,
  wind farm, gas plant or discharging battery connected at a substation
  "injects" power there.

  The opposite is withdrawal, also called offtake or load: power taken out of
  the grid. A data center, factory, city or charging battery withdraws power.

  How the terms appear in our work:
  - Injection capacity (LandGate, Nira and others) is the number of megawatts of
    new generation you could connect at a substation before some line or
    transformer would overload. In our map this is the Generation lens: "new
    generation this bus can take".
  - Offtake capacity is the number of megawatts of new load a substation could
    supply. In our map this is the Large load lens: "new load this bus can serve
    at peak", the data-center question.
  - Net injection is a term inside the model. At every substation it's
    generation minus load in a given scenario. Positive means the bus exports
    power to the grid; negative means it imports. The power-flow calculation
    uses these numbers to work out the flow on every line.

  The same substation can score differently in each direction. A rural
  substation surrounded by wind farms may have no room for more injection,
  because its lines are already full exporting power. The same substation may
  still have plenty of room for offtake, because a data center there would
  consume power locally and relieve those lines. That's why the map shows both
  lenses separately.

  Batteries do both: they withdraw when charging and inject when discharging.

● In simple terms: Nira uses the same detailed grid model the grid operator
  uses, and says its answers almost always match the official results.

  - ISOs are the grid operators that run the regional power grids and the
    interconnection queues, e.g. CAISO in California, PJM in the Mid-Atlantic
    and ERCOT in Texas.
  - Power-flow models are the ISO's detailed computer model of its grid: every
    line, transformer and power plant, with their real ratings. The ISO uses
    this model to decide whether a new project can connect and which upgrades it
    must pay for. Outsiders can only get it under a confidentiality agreement,
    because it's classified as sensitive infrastructure data.
  - The "97% match" claim: Nira runs its own calculations on those same models.
    It says that when it compares its predictions with the official study
    results the ISO later publishes, they agree more than 97% of the time.

  Why it matters for us: this is Nira's main advantage over our map. We
  approximate the grid from public data, with estimated line ratings and no
  outage checks, so our results are a rough screening. Nira's should be much
  closer to what the utility will actually decide.

  Caveat: the 97% figure is Nira's own marketing claim. It's not independently
  verified, and it doesn't say what exactly "match" means: whether a project
  passes, the upgrade costs, or something else. It's a good question for a demo
  or an interview.

> Data-center offtake; claims AC power-flow models

● This is the "strengths" cell for LandGate in the competitor table. It covers
  two things.

  "Data-center offtake": LandGate shows how much power a data center could draw
  from the grid at each substation. That's the offtake (load) capacity from
  earlier, the reverse of injection. With the data-center boom it's in high
  demand, and many competitors still focus mainly on generation.

  "Claims AC power-flow models": this is about how detailed LandGate's grid
  calculations are.
  - DC power flow is a simplified, fast approximation that only tracks how real
    power (MW) splits across the lines. It ignores voltage levels and reactive
    power. Our map uses this method, as do many screening tools.
  - AC power flow is the full physics. It also models voltages and reactive
    power, so it can catch problems a DC model misses, such as voltage dropping
    too low when a large data center is connected. Grid operators use AC models
    in their official studies.

  So LandGate's claim is that its calculations are closer to the physics the
  utilities use than a simplified DC model like ours.

  Caveats:
  - It's LandGate's own marketing claim.
  - A detailed AC model is only as good as its inputs: real line ratings and the
    actual grid model.
  - A competitor (PVcase) says LandGate still skips outage checks (N-1), which
    can matter more than AC versus DC.

> what does this mean?
  "How much will be left after everyone ahead of me?" Our headroom after the
  queue weights every queued request by its odds of reaching operation
  (historically about 20%) before counting what remains. This is part of our

● It describes one specific feature of our map and how it works.

  The question a developer asks: "If I connect at this substation, how much
  capacity will actually be left for me?" Today's free capacity isn't the
  answer, because other projects are already waiting in line (the queue) for the
  same substation. If they get built first, they use up part of that capacity.

  The simple approach most tools take: show today's capacity and ignore the
  queue, or assume every queued project gets built. Neither is realistic,
  because most queued projects never get built: historically only about 1 in 5
  (20%) reached operation, and the rest dropped out.

  What our map does instead: it counts each queued project by how likely it is
  to be built. The weights depend on how far along the project is:
  - early study: 10%
  - late study: 25%
  - interconnection agreement signed: 55%
  - under construction: 90%

  These average out to roughly the 20% historical rate.

  Example:
  - A substation has 300 MW free today.
  - 1,000 MW of projects are queued there, mostly in early study.
  - Counting all 1,000 MW would make it look full.
  - Weighted by their odds, they're expected to use about 150 MW.
  - So the map shows about 150 MW left for you, rather than 300 MW or zero.

  The real model does this through the power-flow calculation across nearby
  lines, not with simple subtraction, but the idea is the same.

  "This is part of our MVP, not a promise" means the feature already exists in
  the map we built. It's not just a plan for the future. That gives the claim
  more credibility in the pitch.
